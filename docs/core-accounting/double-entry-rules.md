## The one equation

Every set of books in Australia, the UK and everywhere else rests on one equation:

> **Assets = Liabilities + Equity**

- **Assets**: what the business owns or controls and expects to benefit from: cash, receivables, stock, equipment.
- **Liabilities**: what it owes: suppliers, the tax office, a bank loan, wages not yet paid.
- **Equity**: what is left for the owners: capital put in, plus profits kept, minus drawings or dividends.

Profit increases equity, so revenue and expenses are really sub-accounts of equity. Expand the equation and you get the version a bookkeeper uses every day:

> **Assets + Expenses = Liabilities + Equity + Revenue**

The left side increases with a **debit**. The right side increases with a **credit**.

## Debits and credits

| Account type | Increase with | Decrease with | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Asset | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Expense | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Liability | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Equity | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Revenue | Credit | Debit | Credit |

"Debit" means left and "credit" means right. Nothing more. A debit is not good or bad.

Every transaction touches **at least two accounts**, and the debits always equal the credits. That is why the books balance: if each entry balances, the total of all entries balances too.

### Worked entries

Buy a laptop for 2,000 on credit (no sales tax, to keep it simple):

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Computer Equipment (asset ↑) | 2,000 | |
| Accounts Payable (liability ↑) | | 2,000 |

Pay the supplier a month later:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts Payable (liability ↓) | 2,000 | |
| Bank (asset ↓) | | 2,000 |

Invoice a customer 5,000 for consulting:

| Account | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts Receivable (asset ↑) | 5,000 | |
| Sales (revenue ↑) | | 5,000 |

A short way to remember the bank account: money **in** is a debit to Bank, money **out** is a credit to Bank. Your bank statement shows the opposite, because it is written from the bank's side: to the bank, your deposit is money it owes you.

## Accruals basis vs cash basis

- **Cash basis**: record income when cash arrives and expenses when cash leaves. Simple, but the profit for a month depends on when people happened to pay.
- **Accruals basis**: record income when it is **earned** and expenses when they are **incurred**, whatever the timing of the cash. This is what every accounting standard requires for financial statements.

The **matching principle** follows from accruals: expenses are recognised in the same period as the revenue they helped earn. The electricity used in March is a March expense even though the bill arrives in April. That is why month-end has accruals, prepayments and deferred revenue (see the month-end checklist).

Tax is a separate question. A small business may be allowed to account for GST or VAT on a cash basis while its financial statements are still on accruals.

## Prudence, materiality and going concern

**Prudence (conservatism).** When things are uncertain, do not overstate assets or income, and do not understate liabilities or expenses. A likely loss (a customer who will not pay) is provided for now. A possible gain (a lawsuit you might win) is not recognised until it is virtually certain. Modern standards talk about *neutrality* rather than deliberate caution, but the practical habit is the same.

**Materiality.** Information is material if leaving it out or getting it wrong could change the decisions of someone reading the accounts. A $40 stapler is not capitalised and depreciated over five years. Materiality is why businesses set a **capitalisation threshold** as an internal policy.

**Going concern.** Accounts are prepared assuming the business will keep operating for the foreseeable future (at least 12 months from the date the accounts are approved). That is why equipment is shown at cost less depreciation, not at what it would fetch in a fire sale. If the business is about to close, the going-concern basis no longer applies and assets are valued on a break-up basis.

**Consistency.** Once a policy is chosen (say, straight-line depreciation), it is applied the same way every year, so periods can be compared. A change of policy is disclosed.

## The trial balance

A trial balance lists every account with its balance in a debit or credit column. If the totals agree, every entry balanced. It does **not** prove the books are right: a bill posted to the wrong expense account, or a transaction missed entirely, still balances.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking "credit" means good or money in. In the bank account, a credit is money **out**.
- Posting a customer receipt to Sales instead of to Accounts Receivable when an invoice was already raised. That counts the income twice.
- Posting a loan repayment entirely to expense. Only the interest is an expense; the principal reduces the liability.
- Treating owner drawings as wages. Drawings reduce equity; they are not an expense of the business.

## In Xero / QBO

You rarely type a debit or credit. When you approve a bill coded to *Rent*, the software debits Rent (and GST or VAT) and credits Accounts Payable for you. Knowing the double entry behind each screen is what lets you fix a wrong balance sheet: you can see which account a mis-posting landed in.
